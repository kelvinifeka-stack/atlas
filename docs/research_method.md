# ATLAS Research Method

## Purpose

ATLAS exists to discover computational principles that improve how humans make evidence-based decisions about the physical world.

We do not begin by building products.

We begin by understanding reality.

Software is the final expression of understanding, not the starting point.

---

# The Atlas Method

Every research investigation follows the same process.

## 1. Observe

Begin with a real-world observation rather than an idea.

Examples:

* Engineers spend significant time transferring information between tools.
* Important assumptions are often buried inside reports.
* Decisions become difficult to audit months after they are made.

An observation is not a conclusion. It is simply something that appears to be true and deserves investigation.

---

## 2. Gather Evidence

Determine whether the observation is widespread.

Evidence may include:

* Academic literature
* Industry standards
* Existing software
* Technical documentation
* Case studies
* Engineering workflows

If an observation cannot be supported by evidence, it remains a hypothesis.

---

## 3. Define the Problem

Reduce the observation to a precise problem statement.

A good problem statement is:

* Specific
* Measurable
* Testable
* Independent of any proposed solution

We avoid jumping directly to implementation.

---

## 4. Study Existing Work

Before proposing anything new, understand what already exists.

For every topic we ask:

* Who has worked on this?
* What mathematical framework do they use?
* What software exists?
* What problems remain unsolved?

Our goal is not originality.

Our goal is understanding.

---

## 5. Form a Hypothesis

Create the simplest explanation consistent with the evidence.

Every hypothesis must include:

* The claim
* Why it may be true
* What evidence supports it
* What evidence could prove it false

If a hypothesis cannot be falsified, it does not belong in Atlas.

---

## 6. Formalize

Translate the hypothesis into a computational or mathematical model.

Possible representations include:

* Graphs
* Logic
* State machines
* Optimization problems
* Probabilistic models
* Algorithms

A hypothesis that cannot be expressed clearly is probably not understood well enough.

---

## 7. Prototype

Build the smallest implementation capable of testing the hypothesis.

The objective is not a polished product.

The objective is learning.

Every prototype exists to answer one research question.

---

## 8. Evaluate

Compare the results with the original hypothesis.

Possible outcomes:

* Supported
* Not Supported
* Inconclusive

Negative results are valuable and must be preserved.

---

## 9. Reflect

Record what changed.

Every completed investigation answers five questions:

1. What did we learn?
2. What surprised us?
3. Which assumptions changed?
4. What remains unknown?
5. What should be investigated next?

---

# Research Principles

Atlas follows these principles in every project.

1. Evidence over intuition.
2. Simplicity over complexity.
3. Truth over attachment.
4. Mathematics before implementation.
5. Build only after understanding.
6. Preserve failed ideas.
7. Every major claim requires evidence.
8. Every hypothesis must be falsifiable.
9. Every experiment must be reproducible.
10. We change our minds when the evidence changes.

---

# Success Criteria

Atlas succeeds when it produces one or more of the following:

* Better explanations
* Better questions
* Better computational models
* Better experiments
* Better engineering tools

Success is not measured by lines of code.

Success is measured by reduced uncertainty.

---

# The Atlas Oath

We will observe carefully.

We will reason rigorously.

We will question our own conclusions.

We will preserve evidence.

We will welcome being proven wrong.

We will build only what survives criticism.

Our goal is not to be first.

Our goal is to be correct.
